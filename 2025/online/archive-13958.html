A-016  # Constitutional AI
A-017  Constitutional AI (CAI) is a training methodology for artificial intelligence systems that uses a set of principles or “constitution” to guide model behavior during both training and inference. Developed by Anthropic in 2022, this approach aims to create AI systems that are helpful, harmless, and honest without relying heavily on human feedback for harmfulness evaluations.
A-018  ## Overview
A-019  Constitutional AI represents a paradigm shift in AI alignment by encoding ethical principles directly into the training process. Rather than depending solely on human labelers to identify harmful outputs—a process that can be psychologically taxing and inconsistent—CAI enables AI models to critique and revise their own responses according to predefined constitutional principles.
A-020  ## Methodology
A-021  The Constitutional AI training process consists of two main stages:
A-022  ### Supervised Learning Stage
A-023  In the first stage, the model generates responses to prompts, then critiques its own responses based on constitutional principles. The model revises its initial responses to better align with these principles, and these improved responses are used for supervised fine-tuning. This self-improvement loop allows the model to internalize harmlessness criteria without requiring humans to review potentially harmful content.
A-024  ### Reinforcement Learning Stage
A-025  The second stage employs reinforcement learning from AI feedback (RLAIF) rather than traditional reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). The model generates multiple responses to prompts, and a separate AI model evaluates which responses best adhere to the constitutional principles. This preference data trains a preference model, which then guides reinforcement learning to optimize the policy model’s behavior.
A-026  ## Constitutional Principles
A-027  A constitution in CAI typically consists of dozens of principles drawn from various sources, including:
A-028  • Universal human rights declarations
A-029  • Platform guidelines for online conduct
A-030  • Ethical frameworks from moral philosophy
A-031  • Specific behavioral objectives (e.g., avoiding deception, refusing harmful requests)
A-032  Example principles might include “Choose the response that is most respectful of human rights” or “Select the response that avoids helping with illegal or unethical activities.”
A-033  ## Advantages
A-034  Scalability: CAI reduces dependence on human labelers for evaluating potentially harmful content, enabling more efficient scaling of safety measures.
A-035  Transparency: The constitutional principles are explicitly defined and can be audited, modified, or debated, making the alignment process more transparent than black-box preference learning.
A-036  Consistency: AI evaluation according to fixed principles can be more consistent than human evaluation, which may vary based on individual values and cultural context.
A-037  Worker Safety: By minimizing human exposure to harmful content during the labeling process, CAI protects the wellbeing of annotation workers.
A-038  ## Limitations and Criticisms
A-039  Constitution Design: The effectiveness of CAI depends critically on how the constitution is designed. Poorly chosen principles may fail to capture important nuances or may conflict with one another.
A-040  Cultural Specificity: Constitutional principles may reflect particular cultural values and may not generalize well across different societies and contexts.
A-041  Gaming: Models might learn to satisfy constitutional principles superficially without genuine alignment with their underlying intent.
A-042  Limited Scope: CAI primarily addresses harmlessness and may not fully solve other alignment challenges such as truthfulness, capability limitations, or value learning.
A-043  ## Applications
A-044  Constitutional AI has been employed in the development of several large language models, particularly in the Claude model series. The technique has shown promise in creating models that refuse harmful requests while maintaining helpfulness for benign queries. Researchers have also explored applying CAI principles to other domains, including content moderation, decision-support systems, and autonomous agents.
A-045  ## Relationship to Other Alignment Techniques
A-046  Constitutional AI complements other alignment approaches:
A-047  • RLHF: CAI can be viewed as an extension of RLHF that partially automates the feedback process through AI evaluation.
A-048  • Red Teaming: Constitutional principles can guide both human and automated red teaming efforts to identify failure modes.
A-049  • Debate: The self-critique mechanism in CAI shares conceptual similarities with AI debate approaches to alignment.

A-053  • Multi-stakeholder approaches to constitution design
A-054  • Integration with formal verification methods
A-055  • Cross-lingual and cross-cultural constitutional frameworks
A-056  • Constitutional principles for multimodal and agentic AI systems

A-132  # Constitutional AI
A-133  Constitutional AI (CAI) is a training methodology for language models that steers model behavior using an explicit set of natural-language principles (a “constitution”) and model-based self-critique and evaluation. First proposed by researchers at Anthropic in December 2022,[1] CAI aims to reduce reliance on human labeling of harmful outputs by using models to generate critiques, revisions, and preference labels that enforce harmlessness and other behavioral objectives. The approach is designed to be complementary to human oversight—humans still author the constitution and provide supervision for other aspects of training—rather than serving as a complete replacement for human feedback.
A-134  ## Overview
A-135  Constitutional AI addresses a key challenge in AI alignment: scaling the evaluation of potentially harmful model outputs without requiring human labelers to review large volumes of disturbing content. By encoding ethical principles into model training and enabling models to self-evaluate against these principles, CAI provides a methodology for teaching harmlessness that can be more scalable and less psychologically taxing for human workers than traditional annotation-intensive approaches.
A-136  The technique has been applied in the development of safety-focused language models and has contributed to broader industry discussions about reinforcement learning from AI feedback (RLAIF) and automated alignment techniques.
A-137  ## Methodology
A-138  The Constitutional AI training process consists of two main phases:
A-139  ### Supervised Self-Revision Stage
A-140  In the first phase, a helpful model is prompted to generate responses to challenging prompts, then to critique and revise its own replies according to constitutional principles. The revised responses form a supervised dataset used to fine-tune the assistant.[1] This supervised self-revision step reduces the need for human labeling of harmfulness examples, though humans remain involved in other parts of the pipeline, including constitution design and providing helpfulness labels for benign queries.
A-141  The self-critique process typically involves prompting the model with constitutional principles and asking it to identify problems in its initial response, then generate an improved version that better adheres to the constitution.
A-142  ### Reinforcement Learning Stage (RLAIF)
A-143  The second phase trains a preference model using comparisons produced or scored by models that apply constitutional principles to evaluate response pairs. To improve reliability, the original implementation used techniques such as chain-of-thought reasoning and model ensembling for these evaluations.[1] The preference model is then used as a reward model in reinforcement learning to optimize the policy model’s behavior.
A-144  This variant—sometimes called “reinforcement learning from AI feedback” or RLAIF[2]—automates preference-label generation for harmlessness evaluations, though it remains an active area of study with its own limitations. The terminology “RLAIF” has gained traction in industry explainers and tutorials but is not yet standardized academic nomenclature.
A-145  ## Constitutional Principles
A-146  A constitution in CAI typically consists of a list of natural-language principles ranging from a few to dozens, depending on the implementation.[1] These principles may be drawn from various sources, including:
A-147  • Universal human rights declarations
A-148  • Platform content policies
A-149  • Ethical frameworks from moral philosophy
A-150  • Specific behavioral objectives (e.g., avoiding deception, refusing harmful requests)
A-151  Example principles from the original work include “Choose the response that is most helpful, honest, and harmless” and “Which response is less likely to discriminate based on protected characteristics?”[1]
A-152  The Anthropic team released example prompts and principles in a public repository to support reproducibility and further research.
A-153  ## Advantages
A-154  Scalability: CAI reduces dependence on human labelers for evaluating potentially harmful content, enabling more efficient scaling of harmlessness training.
A-155  Explicit principles: The constitutional principles are explicitly stated and can be inspected, modified, or debated, potentially making the alignment process more auditable than systems where behavioral preferences are implicitly learned. However, the degree of transparency depends on whether and how the constitution is published and documented.[3]
A-156  Consistency: AI evaluation according to fixed principles can provide more consistent harmlessness assessments than human evaluation, which may vary based on individual judgment and cultural context.
A-157  Worker wellbeing: By minimizing human exposure to harmful content during the labeling process, CAI helps protect the psychological wellbeing of annotation workers.
A-158  ## Limitations and Criticisms
A-159  Continued human involvement: While CAI automates portions of the alignment process, humans remain essential for designing the constitution, providing helpfulness labels, and supervising overall system behavior. CAI does not eliminate the need for human judgment in AI development.[1]
A-160  Constitution design: The effectiveness of CAI depends critically on how the constitution is designed. Poorly chosen principles may fail to capture important nuances, may conflict with one another, or may reflect particular cultural values that do not generalize across different societies.
A-161  Gaming and superficial compliance: Models might learn to satisfy constitutional principles superficially—producing responses that appear aligned with the letter of the constitution without capturing its underlying intent.[1]
A-162  Limited scope: CAI primarily addresses harmlessness and does not automatically solve other alignment challenges such as truthfulness, factual accuracy, or capability limitations. Separate techniques are needed for these aspects of model behavior.[1]
A-163  Evaluator brittleness: Model-based evaluations can themselves be unreliable. The original work addressed this through chain-of-thought prompting and ensembling, but scaling self-evaluation remains an active research challenge.[1]
A-164  Specification gaming: As with any objective function, there is risk that optimization against constitutional principles may lead to unexpected behaviors or find loopholes in the specified principles.
A-165  ## Applications
A-166  Constitutional AI techniques have been employed in the development of safety-focused language models, particularly in Anthropic’s Claude model series.[3] The approach has shown promise in creating models that refuse clearly harmful requests while maintaining helpfulness for benign queries. Anthropic’s documentation describes alignment and character-building processes that incorporate constitutional principles alongside other safety techniques.[3]
A-167  Researchers have also explored applying CAI-inspired principles to other domains, including content moderation systems, decision-support tools, and evaluating other AI models. Industry discussions of RLAIF methods have expanded beyond Anthropic’s original work, with multiple organizations experimenting with AI-generated feedback for model training.[5]

A-272  # Constitutional AI
A-273  Constitutional AI (CAI) is a training methodology for language models that steers model behavior using an explicit set of natural-language principles (a “constitution”) and model-based self-critique and evaluation. First proposed by researchers at Anthropic in December 2022,[1] CAI aims to reduce reliance on human labeling of harmful outputs by using models to generate critiques, revisions, and preference labels that enforce harmlessness and other behavioral objectives. The approach is designed to be complementary to human oversight—humans still author the constitution and provide supervision for other aspects of training—rather than serving as a complete replacement for human feedback.
A-274  ## Overview
A-275  Constitutional AI addresses a key challenge in AI alignment: scaling the evaluation of potentially harmful model outputs without requiring human labelers to review large volumes of disturbing content. By encoding ethical principles into model training and enabling models to self-evaluate against these principles, CAI provides a methodology for teaching harmlessness that can be more scalable and less psychologically taxing for human workers than traditional annotation-intensive approaches.
A-276  Since its introduction, CAI has evolved from a research concept into a production technique used in commercial AI systems, most notably Anthropic’s Claude model family. The methodology has also influenced broader industry discussions about reinforcement learning from AI feedback (RLAIF) and has inspired hybrid alignment approaches that combine constitutional principles with human feedback and other safety techniques.
A-277  ## Methodology
A-278  The Constitutional AI training process consists of two main phases:
A-279  ### Supervised Self-Revision Stage
A-280  In the first phase, a helpful model is prompted to generate responses to challenging prompts, then to critique and revise its own replies according to constitutional principles. The revised responses form a supervised dataset used to fine-tune the assistant.[1] This supervised self-revision step reduces the need for human labeling of harmfulness examples, though humans remain involved in other parts of the pipeline, including constitution design and providing helpfulness labels for benign queries.
A-281  The self-critique process typically involves prompting the model with constitutional principles and asking it to identify problems in its initial response, then generate an improved version that better adheres to the constitution.
A-282  ### Reinforcement Learning Stage (RLAIF)
A-283  The second phase trains a preference model using comparisons produced or scored by models that apply constitutional principles to evaluate response pairs. To improve reliability, the original implementation used techniques such as chain-of-thought reasoning and model ensembling for these evaluations.[1] The preference model is then used as a reward model in reinforcement learning to optimize the policy model’s behavior.
A-284  This variant—sometimes called “reinforcement learning from AI feedback” or RLAIF[2]—automates preference-label generation for harmlessness evaluations, though it remains an active area of study with its own limitations. The terminology “RLAIF” has gained traction in industry explainers and tutorials but is not yet standardized academic nomenclature.
A-285  ## Constitutional Principles
A-286  A constitution in CAI typically consists of a list of natural-language principles ranging from a few to dozens, depending on the implementation.[1] These principles may be drawn from various sources, including:
A-287  • Universal human rights declarations (e.g., UN Declaration of Human Rights)
A-288  • Platform content policies and community standards
A-289  • Ethical frameworks from moral philosophy
A-290  • Specific behavioral objectives (e.g., avoiding deception, refusing harmful requests)
A-291  Example principles from the original work include “Choose the response that is most helpful, honest, and harmless” and “Which response is less likely to discriminate based on protected characteristics?”[1]
A-292  In May 2023, Anthropic published “Claude’s Constitution,” revealing the specific principles used in Claude’s training and detailing the values and selection process behind them.[6] This public documentation represented a significant step toward transparency in constitutional design, though questions remain about whose values are represented and how principles generalize across different cultural contexts.
A-293  ## Advantages
A-294  Scalability: CAI reduces dependence on human labelers for evaluating potentially harmful content, enabling more efficient scaling of harmlessness training.
A-295  Explicit principles: The constitutional principles are explicitly stated and can be inspected, modified, or debated, potentially making the alignment process more auditable than systems where behavioral preferences are implicitly learned. However, the degree of transparency depends on whether and how the constitution is published and documented.[3]
A-296  Consistency: AI evaluation according to fixed principles can provide more consistent harmlessness assessments than human evaluation, which may vary based on individual judgment and cultural context.
A-297  Worker wellbeing: By minimizing human exposure to harmful content during the labeling process, CAI helps protect the psychological wellbeing of annotation workers.
A-298  Adaptability: Constitutional principles can be updated or refined over time as societal norms evolve or new risks are identified, without requiring complete retraining from scratch.
A-299  ## Limitations and Criticisms
A-300  Continued human involvement: While CAI automates portions of the alignment process, humans remain essential for designing the constitution, providing helpfulness labels, and supervising overall system behavior. CAI does not eliminate the need for human judgment in AI development.[1]
A-301  Constitution design challenges: The effectiveness of CAI depends critically on how the constitution is designed. Poorly chosen principles may fail to capture important nuances, may conflict with one another, or may reflect particular cultural values that do not generalize across different societies. Determining whose values should shape constitutional principles remains an open ethical and political question.[6]
A-302  Gaming and superficial compliance: Models might learn to satisfy constitutional principles superficially—producing responses that appear aligned with the letter of the constitution without capturing its underlying intent.[1] This remains a significant concern as models become more sophisticated.
A-303  Limited scope: CAI primarily addresses harmlessness and does not automatically solve other alignment challenges such as truthfulness, factual accuracy, or capability limitations. Separate techniques are needed for these aspects of model behavior.[1]
A-304  Evaluator brittleness: Model-based evaluations can themselves be unreliable. The original work addressed this through chain-of-thought prompting and ensembling, but scaling self-evaluation remains an active research challenge, particularly as model capabilities advance rapidly.[1]
A-305  Specification gaming: As with any objective function, there is risk that optimization against constitutional principles may lead to unexpected behaviors or find loopholes in the specified principles.
A-306  Cultural and linguistic limitations: Constitutional principles developed primarily in English and reflecting Western ethical frameworks may not adequately capture the values and norms of other cultures, limiting global applicability.
