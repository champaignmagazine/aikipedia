A-071  ## 1. Test-Time Compute Scaling
A-072  Aikipedia Entry — 2025 Edition
A-073  ### Definition
A-074  Test-Time Compute Scaling refers to methods that improve AI model performance by allocating additional computational resources during inference rather than only during training. Instead of generating responses immediately, models invest extra time in iterative reasoning, self-correction, verification, and exploration of multiple solution paths before producing final outputs.
A-075  ### Historical Context
A-076  Traditional language models operated on a simple paradigm: generate outputs as quickly as possible using fixed inference budgets. Pre-training scaling laws dominated the field from 2018–2024, with performance improvements achieved primarily by training larger models on more data.
A-077  In September 2024, OpenAI released the o1 model series, which demonstrated that performance consistently improves with more time spent thinking during inference. This marked a fundamental shift: as the field encountered data bottlenecks and diminishing returns from pre-training scaling laws, test-time compute emerged as a key driver of performance improvements.
A-078  By early 2025, the paradigm had crystallized with models like DeepSeek-R1 exploring reinforcement learning approaches to achieve strong reasoning through deep, step-by-step thinking. Multiple research groups published frameworks for achieving test-time scaling, making it accessible beyond closed proprietary systems.
A-079  ### Technical Description
A-080  Test-time compute encompasses several interconnected approaches:
A-081  1. Extended Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Reasoning
A-082  Models generate explicit reasoning traces before producing answers, allowing them to work through complex multi-step problems systematically.
A-083  2. Iterative Self-Refinement
A-084  The model generates an initial response, critiques it, identifies errors, and produces improved versions through multiple refinement cycles.
A-085  3. Best-of-N Sampling with Verification
A-086  • Generate N candidate solutions
A-087  • Evaluate each using a reward model (Outcome-Supervised Reward Model for complete solutions, or Process-Supervised Reward Model for step-by-step evaluation)
A-088  • Select the highest-scoring response
A-089  4. Tree Search Strategies
A-090  Models explore multiple reasoning paths simultaneously, backtrack when needed, and search for optimal solutions through the space of possible reasoning chains.
A-091  5. Budget Forcing
A-092  A technique that controls test-time compute by forcefully terminating or lengthening the model’s thinking process by appending “Wait” tokens multiple times, leading the model to double-check its answer and often fix incorrect reasoning steps.
A-093  ### Mathematical Framework
A-094  For a model policy π and input x, traditional inference produces:
A-095  y = π(x)
A-096  Test-time scaling introduces compute budget C:
A-097  y = argmax_{y ∈ 𝒴(x, C)} R(y)
A-098  where 𝒴(x, C)is the set of candidate responses generated using compute budget C, and R is a reward/verification function.
A-099  The performance of o1 consistently improves with more reinforcement learning (train-time compute) and with more time spent thinking (test-time compute).
A-100  ### Notable Systems / Papers (2025)
A-101  • OpenAI o1 & o3: Proprietary reasoning models that demonstrated dramatic improvements on challenging benchmarks through extended thinking time
A-102  • s1 (Muennighoff et al., 2025): Achieved test-time scaling with just 1,000 training examples and budget forcing, exceeding o1-preview on competition math by up to 27%
A-103  • DeepSeek-R1: Leveraged reinforcement learning to achieve strong reasoning through step-by-step thinking
A-104  • Survey by Ji et al. (2025): Comprehensive review organizing test-time compute from System-1 to System-2 thinking paradigms
A-105  • Zeng et al. (ACL 2025): Found that parallel scaling strategies outperform sequential ones, proposing “Shortest Majority Vote” method
A-106  ### Applications
A-107  • Mathematical Problem Solving: o1 achieved 93% on AIME 2024 with extended inference, placing it among the top 500 students nationally
A-108  • Scientific Reasoning: Outperforming PhD-level experts on specialized knowledge benchmarks
A-109  • Code Generation: Testing and iteratively improving generated code before returning results
A-110  • Multi-Step Planning: Breaking complex tasks into verified sub-steps with correction loops
A-111  • Safety-Critical Decisions: Medical diagnosis, legal analysis where accuracy matters more than speed
A-112  ### Limitations
A-113  • Increased Latency: Users must wait longer for responses, unsuitable for real-time applications
A-114  • Higher Computational Cost: More expensive to run, requiring careful cost-quality tradeoffs
A-115  • Diminishing Returns: Research shows that longer chains of thought don’t always enhance accuracy; correct solutions are often shorter than incorrect ones

A-125  ## 2. Compound AI Systems
A-126  Aikipedia Entry — 2025 Edition
A-127  ### Definition
A-128  Compound AI Systems are systems that tackle AI tasks using multiple interacting components, including multiple calls to models, retrievers, or external tools. Rather than relying on a single monolithic model, these systems orchestrate specialized components—language models, retrieval systems, code executors, databases, and domain-specific tools—into integrated workflows.
A-129  ### Historical Context
A-130  The Berkeley Artificial Intelligence Research (BAIR) blog published a seminal analysis in February 2024 defining compound AI systems and arguing they would be one of the most impactful trends in AI.
A-131  Throughout 2024, the paradigm gained momentum: 60% of enterprise LLM applications used retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), and 30% used multi-step chains. By 2025, compound systems had become the dominant architecture for production AI applications, with dedicated research on optimization, resource management, and system design emerging rapidly.
A-132  In April 2025, Groq launched Compound Beta, its first compound AI system combining LLMs with web search and code execution capabilities, demonstrating mainstream adoption of the paradigm.
A-133  ### Technical Description
A-134  Compound AI Systems consist of multiple specialized components orchestrated through control logic:
A-135  Core Components:
A-136  • Foundation Models: One or more LLMs for reasoning, planning, and language understanding
A-137  • Retrieval Systems: Vector databases, traditional search engines, or knowledge graphs for external information
A-138  • Tool Interfaces: APIs, code executors, calculators, databases, and domain-specific services
A-139  • Orchestration Layer: Control logic that routes requests, manages state, and coordinates component interactions
A-140  • Verification/Critique Systems: Components that evaluate and refine outputs from other parts of the system
A-141  System Architecture Patterns:
A-142  • Sequential Chaining: Components execute in a predetermined order (e.g., retrieve → reason → generate)
A-143  • Dynamic Routing: The system decides which components to invoke based on input characteristics
A-144  • Iterative Refinement: Results cycle through multiple components for improvement
A-145  • Parallel Execution: Multiple components process simultaneously, results aggregated at the end
A-146  ### Why Compound Systems?
A-147  Some tasks are easier to improve via system design than by scaling models alone. For example, suppose the best LLM solves coding problems 30% of the time, and tripling its training budget increases this to 35%; in contrast, engineering a system that samples multiple times and tests each solution might increase performance to 80%.
A-148  Performance goals vary widely—each AI model has a fixed quality level and cost, but applications often need to vary these parameters. Compound systems enable precise cost-quality tradeoffs impossible with single models.

A-173  ## 3. Mechanistic Interpretability Tools
A-174  Aikipedia Entry — 2025 Edition
A-175  ### Definition
A-176  Mechanistic Interpretability aims to understand the computational mechanisms underlying neural networks’ capabilities in order to accomplish concrete scientific and engineering goals. Mechanistic Interpretability Tools are the suite of techniques, software libraries, and methodologies developed to reverse-engineer neural networks by analyzing their internal components—weights, activations, attention patterns, and learned features—to reveal how they perform computations and represent knowledge.
A-177  ### Historical Context
A-178  Interpretability research initially focused on behavioral approaches: testing model inputs and outputs to understand capabilities. However, this provided limited insight into internal mechanisms.
A-179  The mechanistic interpretability paradigm emerged around 2020–2022 with work on “circuits”—identifying specific neural network components responsible for particular capabilities. The field grew rapidly, with 140+ papers submitted to the ICML 2024 mechanistic interpretability workshop, dedicated startups, and a rich ecosystem of tools.
A-180  In January 2025, a major review paper “Open Problems in Mechanistic Interpretability” synthesized the field’s frontier and open challenges, signaling its maturation. By 2025, mechanistic interpretability had transitioned from research curiosity to practical tool for AI safety, debugging, and system understanding.
A-181  ### Technical Description
A-182  Core Methodological Approaches:
A-183  1. Circuit Discovery
A-184  Identifying minimal subgraphs of the network that implement specific behaviors:
A-185  • Activation Patching: Systematically replacing activations to determine which components are causally important
A-186  • Attribution Methods: Computing gradients or attention flow to trace information pathways
A-187  • Ablation Studies: Removing components to measure their contribution
A-188  2. Feature Extraction
A-189  Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs): The most prominent tool in 2025, SAEs learn interpretable feature representations by decomposing neural activations into sparse combinations of learned features. This addresses the “superposition” problem where networks represent many features in fewer dimensions.
A-190  3. Representation Analysis
A-191  • Probing: Training classifiers on internal activations to detect what information is encoded
A-192  • Similarity Metrics: Measuring how representations change across layers or training time
A-193  • Causal Interventions: Directly editing representations to test their causal role
A-194  4. Visualization Techniques
A-195  • Feature Visualization: Generating inputs that maximally activate specific neurons or features
A-196  • Attention Maps: Visualizing which tokens the model attends to during processing
A-197  • Activation Atlases: Creating searchable maps of internal representations
A-198  ### Software Ecosystem (2025)
A-199  TransformerLens: Best for small models ≤9B where researchers want to write complex interpretability experiments; version 3 released in September 2025 works well with large models and is far more flexible
A-200  nnsight: More performant, works well on larger models, serving as a wrapper around standard LLM libraries like HuggingFace transformers
A-201  ARENA: Open-source tutorial for learning key mechanistic interpretability concepts and techniques
A-202  Neuronpedia: Web platform for exploring and sharing discovered interpretable features
A-203  Prisma: Open-source toolkit specifically designed for mechanistic interpretability in vision and video models
A-204  ### Major Research Threads (2025)
A-205  Workshops at NeurIPS 2025 and CVPR 2025 focused on mechanistic interpretability, showcasing cutting-edge research and fostering cross-pollination between methodological traditions
A-206  Anthropic published “Circuits Updates” in April 2025, sharing emerging research strands including work on jailbreak mechanisms and dense latents in residual stream SAEs
A-207  The Mechanistic Interpretability Benchmark (MIB) was introduced with two tracks: circuit localization and causal variable localization, spanning four tasks and five models to evaluate interpretability methods
A-208  ### Applications
A-209  AI Safety: Detecting deception, understanding failure modes, and verifying alignment properties
A-210  Model Debugging: Anthropic used interpretability to examine jailbreaks and understand why models partially comply with harmful requests
A-211  Capability Enhancement: Identifying and amplifying useful circuits while suppressing unwanted behaviors
A-212  Scientific Understanding: Shedding light on exciting scientific questions about the nature of intelligence
A-213  Efficient Model Design: Understanding which components are essential allows for better compression and architecture design
A-214  Vision Models: Mechanistic interpretability expanded significantly into computer vision in 2025, with dedicated workshops and tools for understanding visual processing
A-215  ### Limitations
A-216  Non-Identifiability: Research reveals multiple circuits can replicate model behavior, multiple interpretations can exist for a circuit, and several algorithms can be causally aligned with the network
A-217  Scalability: Techniques developed on small models often don’t scale efficiently to frontier models with hundreds of billions of parameters
A-218  Completeness: Even when we understand specific circuits, we may lack a complete picture of how they interact
A-219  Validation: Difficult to verify whether our interpretations are correct rather than post-hoc rationalizations
A-220  Complexity: Modern models exhibit extreme complexity, making comprehensive reverse-engineering intractable
A-221  ### Open Questions
A-222  • How can we improve our methods both conceptually and practically to reveal deeper insights?
A-223  • How do we scale mechanistic interpretability techniques to models with trillions of parameters?
A-224  • Can we develop automated interpretability that discovers circuits without human guidance?
A-225  • Is the uniqueness of explanations essential for understanding, or can we adopt pragmatic standards requiring only predictive and manipulability criteria?
A-226  • How do mechanistic interpretability insights from language models transfer to other domains like vision, audio, and multi-modal systems?
