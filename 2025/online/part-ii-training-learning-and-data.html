Corrected full entry — Game-Theoretic Statistics (L5-D10)

Revised entry by DeepSeek-V3.2 (corrected canonical text; C02 applied). Source text: A-110 to A-116.

Definition

A framework for sequential and anytime-valid inference that reimagines statistical testing as a gambling game. It uses e-values and e-processes (non-negative martingales that represent a gambler's wealth) to provide statistical evidence that remains valid regardless of when or why you stop collecting data (a practice known as "optional stopping").

2025 Context

While building on foundational work by Shafer, Vovk, and others, this framework saw renewed emphasis and discussion in 2025 for its application to modern AI challenges. It was the subject of an ICML 2025 tutorial, Aaditya Ramdas's "Game-theoretic Statistics and Sequential Anytime-Valid Inference" (July 14, 2025), which presented it as an alternative to classical p-values, which can be invalidated by the common practice of adaptively analyzing data. [C02]

Implication

Game-Theoretic Statistics addresses a critical need in machine learning and science, where data often arrives sequentially and analyses are performed adaptively. By providing robust statistical guarantees for continuous monitoring and adaptive experimentation, it offers a more reliable foundation for A/B testing, safety monitoring of deployed AI systems, and reinforcement learning, where decisions must be made under uncertainty with streaming data.

That wording replaced a citation of the ICML 2025 tutorial after a review called it unverifiable. The tutorial existed, so the citation is restored: Ramdas, ICML 2025, July 14, 2025, https://icml.cc/virtual/2025/tutorial/40002.) [C02]

1. Definition

On-policy distillation is a training paradigm for large language models (LLMs) where a student model generates trajectories under its own policy (i.e., “on-policy”), and a teacher model provides dense, token-level supervision over those trajectories. The student then updates its parameters to align its probability distribution with the teacher’s, typically via a reverse KL divergence loss. This combines the distributional relevance of on-policy reinforcement learning (RL) with the dense feedback of distillation. (Thinking Machines Lab, 2025) [R1]

2. Origin and Historical Development

On-policy distillation of language models was proposed before 2025. Agarwal et al. introduced Generalized Knowledge Distillation (GKD), which trains a student on its own sampled output sequences using the teacher's token-level probabilities (arXiv, June 23, 2023; ICLR 2024; titled "On-Policy Distillation of Language Models" from January 2024) [P1]. Gu et al.'s MiniLLM distilled large language models by minimizing the reverse KL divergence (arXiv, June 14, 2023; ICLR 2024) [P2]. The Thinking Machines Lab post of October 27, 2025 presents its work as extending these and the Qwen3 team's use of the method [R1] [C02].

3. Conceptual or Technical Framework
3.1 Background

LLM development usually proceeds in three phases:

  1. Pre-training — broad language and knowledge acquisition.
  2. Mid-training — domain adaptation (e.g., code, legal, or medical corpora).
  3. Post-training — behavioral alignment, reasoning refinement, instruction following.

Traditional post-training uses either:

  • Off-policy learning (SFT / distillation): training on data or trajectories generated by another model.
  • On-policy RL: sampling from the model’s own policy and optimizing sparse rewards.

Both have trade-offs:

Approach Strength Weakness
Off-policy SFT/Distillation Dense supervision Distributional mismatch (student sees unseen states)
On-policy RL Realistic trajectories Sparse reward → inefficient learning

On-policy distillation resolves this tension: it trains on the student’s visited states while using dense, per-token feedback from a strong teacher.

3.2 Methodology

  1. Trajectory Generation: The student πθ\pi_\theta samples sequences x1:Tx_{1:T} from its current policy. [C03]

  2. Teacher Evaluation: The teacher πT\pi_T computes log-probabilities logπT(xt∣x<t)\log \pi_T(x_t \mid x_{<t}) for each token generated by the student. [C03]

  3. Policy Alignment: The student minimizes a divergence between its distribution and the teacher’s over the same visited states:

    ℒon-policy=𝔼x1:T∼πθ∑t=1TDKL(πθ(⋅∣x<t)∥πT(⋅∣x<t))\mathcal{L}_{\text{on-policy}} = \mathbb{E}_{x_{1:T} \sim \pi_\theta} \sum_{t=1}^{T} D_{\text{KL}}\!\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x_{<t}) \,\|\, \pi_T(\cdot \mid x_{<t})\big)

    where DKL(p∥q)=∑xp(x)logp(x)q(x)D_{\text{KL}}(p \,\|\, q) = \sum_x p(x)\log \tfrac{p(x)}{q(x)}. [C03] In practice, the reverse KL encourages the student to sharpen its distribution toward the teacher’s mode while maintaining its own trajectory support.

  4. Gradient Update: Gradients are propagated through the student; the teacher is frozen. Training proceeds over multiple on-policy rollouts. [C07]

3.3 Conceptual intuition

  • Dense feedback, realistic states: Each token prediction yields a learning signal; no need to wait for an episodic reward.

  • Bridging RL + SFT: Mathematically, on-policy distillation resembles policy gradient with a KL-based reward:

    At=−DKL(πθ(⋅∣st)∥πT(⋅∣st))A_t = -D_{\text{KL}}\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s_t) \,\|\, \pi_T(\cdot \mid s_t)\big) [C03]

    making it a hybrid between imitation learning (DAGGER) and reward-based fine-tuning.

3.4 Figure 1 (original)

The image (post attachment 13898) is kept in the archive. Comparative overview of off-policy, on-policy, and on-policy distillation paradigms. The illustration contrasts how trajectories are generated and supervised across three learning setups for large language models.

  • Off-policy: The teacher (policy πₜ) generates trajectories; the student learns from them → risk of distributional shift.
  • On-policy: The agent (policy π_θ) generates its own trajectories and learns from sparse reward → high relevance but low information density.
  • On-policy Distillation: The student samples its own trajectories (on-policy) while a teacher provides dense, token-level feedback → combines realism and efficiency.
4. Applications and Implementations
  • Post-domain fine-tuning recovery: when instruction-following degraded after specialized corpus adaptation, on-policy distillation with the pre-adaptation model as teacher restored near-original performance while preserving new knowledge.
  • Compatibility: works with frozen or external teacher models.
5. Evidence and Evaluation

From Thinking Machines Lab (2025):

  • A Qwen3-8B-Base student, at 60% on AIME'24 after supervised fine-tuning, reached 70% after about 150 steps of on-policy distillation. The post's setup names Qwen3-32B as the teacher; it reports that the on-policy run used Qwen3-8B, which performed slightly better, while counting compute as for the 32B model. The post estimates the method at about 9–30× less compute than extending supervised fine-tuning. Replicating the Qwen3 team's result, it reports on-policy distillation reaching RL-level performance at roughly a tenth of RL's GPU hours [R1] [C04].
6. Significance and Implications
  • High information efficiency: each token provides supervision bits.
  • Reduced distributional shift: learning occurs within the student’s state distribution.
  • Faster convergence: achieves near-teacher performance with far less compute than RL or extended supervised fine-tuning. [C01]
  • Recoverability: can “restore” lost alignment after domain fine-tuning.

On-policy distillation elegantly integrates reinforcement learning’s state realism with distillation’s dense supervision. By aligning a student’s on-policy trajectories to a teacher’s per-token distribution, it enables efficient, compute-light behavioral alignment — accelerating the path toward scalable and adaptive language models.

7. Limitations, Debates, and Open Questions
  • Requires a teacher that outputs reliable token-level probabilities.
  • If the student’s initial policy is far from the teacher’s, guidance may be ineffective.
  • May not discover novel behaviors beyond the teacher’s distribution (unlike exploratory RL).

Note on terminology. The coinage, in the Tülu 3 report, expands the acronym as "Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards"; a large share of the subsequent literature writes "from Verifiable Rewards" instead, and the two expansions are used interchangeably. RLVR names the source of the reward, not the training algorithm and not the training machinery around it: the policy optimizer (PPO, GRPO, and their variants) and auxiliary components such as a learned value model are separate choices, and learned components may serve the training process without supplying the reward. The phrase should also be distinguished from the older, generic practice of training against checked answers — rejection sampling on correct solutions and verifier-scored mathematics predate the acronym. What appeared in late 2024 was the name, and with it a self-conscious recipe.

1. Definition

RLVR is a post-training method for language models in which the reinforcement-learning reward is computed automatically from a specified check on the model's output — a verification function — rather than by a learned reward model trained on human or AI preferences. The checked unit is a complete submitted artifact: a final answer that matches a known solution, a program that passes its unit tests, a full proof that a proof assistant accepts, a response that satisfies a testable formatting constraint. What the reward measures, precisely, is satisfaction of the specified check. That objectivity is real but relative to the specification: a check can be incomplete, brittle, or narrower than the task it stands for, so a verified reward certifies that the output passed the test, not that it is good in every respect the task intends. This is still a sharp difference from preference training, where the reward is a learned model's judgment and can be negotiated with, flattered, or overfitted — but a verifier can be gamed too, at the level of its specification, and the debates in §7 return to that.

2. Origin and Historical Development

Before the name (to 2023). Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) had made the learned reward model the default source of training signal: a model trained on preference judgments stands in for the quality of an output. Training against automatically checked outcomes existed at the margins — sampling candidate solutions and keeping those whose final answers verified, or scoring mathematical solutions against known results — but it was treated as a data-filtering trick, not as the training signal itself. Process supervision (Lightman et al., 2023) marked the adjacent path: reward models trained on human-labeled step correctness, still learned models, still proxies.

February 2024 — the optimizer arrives first. DeepSeekMath introduced Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO): sample a group of responses to one prompt, score each, and use the group's mean score as the baseline, eliminating the separate value network that PPO-style training requires. GRPO's rewards in that work were substantially rule-based correctness signals on mathematics. A cheap way to turn scores into advantages was therefore in place before the term, waiting for a recipe to name the combination.

November 2024 — the coinage. Ai2 announced Tülu 3 publicly on November 21, 2024, its technical announcement introducing "Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR)" by name [R1]; the full report followed on arXiv the next day (Lambert et al., November 22, 2024) [P1], presenting a fully open post-training recipe — supervised fine-tuning, then Direct Preference Optimization, then a final stage it named "a novel method we call Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR)." Tülu 3's verifiers covered domains where correctness is decidable by program: mathematics with known answers, and precise instruction following, where constraints such as length, format, and required content can be tested mechanically. Its implementation is worth recording exactly, because later usage blurred it: Tülu 3's RLVR stage was trained with PPO, including a learned value model to estimate advantages, while the reward came solely from verification; the group-relative alternative that later became standard appears in that work only as a direction for the future. The contribution was as much lexical as technical: a name under which scattered practice could be recognized as one method.

January 2025 — crystallization. DeepSeek-R1 (January 2025; journal version in Nature, September 17, 2025) supplied the demonstration the term needed. Its R1-Zero variant applied GRPO with two rule-based rewards — accuracy of the final answer and compliance with a response format — directly to the DeepSeek-V3 base model, with no supervised fine-tuning stage preceding the RL. Training response lengths grew by orders of magnitude, and self-verification and revision behaviors were observed to emerge over the course of training. Two cautions belong with that observation. First, "no supervised fine-tuning" is not "no supervision": the run began from a pretrained base model carrying everything pretraining had taught it, and the rule-based rewards and the required response format are themselves supervisory structure. Second, that the behaviors appeared during RL does not by itself establish that they are novel capabilities rather than pre-existing ones made more probable — which is precisely the question the mid-2025 critique literature took up. The full R1 added a supervised cold start and further RL stages, but the field's attention fixed on R1-Zero: reasoning behavior elicited under verifiable reward alone. Moonshot AI's Kimi k1.5 report, the same month, was a separate demonstration in the same direction: large-scale RL on reasoning tasks with outcome-based rewards, long-context scaling to 128k tokens, and a framework that pointedly avoided Monte Carlo tree search, value functions, and process reward models, reaching AIME 77.5 and MATH-500 96.2.

Spring 2025 — open replication. Because R1's recipe was only partly documented, the following months produced open reconstructions that doubled as the method's engineering literature. ByteDance Seed's DAPO (March 2025), built on the veRL framework, scored 50 points on AIME 2024 with a Qwen2.5-32B base model — the figure is avg@32, mean accuracy over 32 sampled responses per problem — against 47 (avg@32) for DeepSeek's R1-Zero-Qwen-32B, and reached it in roughly half the training steps, using an unadorned +1/−1 ground-truth reward; both figures are as reported by the DAPO authors. DAPO also documented the four algorithmic details (decoupled clipping, dynamic sampling, token-level loss, overlong-response reward shaping) on which success actually depended. A wave of further reproductions followed; taken together, the named systems span Ai2, DeepSeek, Moonshot AI, and ByteDance Seed — independent organizations that do not share recipes — which supports describing the method as reproducible across laboratories, without claiming a surveyed industry default.

Mid-2025 — scrutiny. Two results complicated the founding story within the year. Yue et al. (April 2025), measuring RLVR-trained models against their base models with pass@k at large k across several model families, RL algorithms, and mathematics, coding, and visual-reasoning benchmarks, found the RL models' advantage confined to small sample counts: given enough samples, base models matched or exceeded them, indicating that the reasoning paths RLVR rewards were already present in the base models' sampling distributions. Their conclusion — that current RLVR training concentrates probability on what pretraining made reachable rather than extending the reachable set — is an empirical finding about the training setups studied, not a proof that verifiable-reward training cannot produce new capability; it is stated here as its authors scoped it. Shao et al. (June 2025) then showed that on Qwen2.5-Math models, even spurious rewards — random, format-only, or computed from incorrect labels — recovered most of the ground-truth gain (MATH-500: +21.4 points from random reward against +29.1 from ground truth), an effect traced to amplification of the family's pre-existing "code reasoning" prior and absent in Llama and OLMo models. Neither result displaced the method; together they reframed what its gains are evidence of.

Late 2025 — consolidation and extension. The R1 paper's appearance in Nature (September 2025) marked the method's passage into the settled record. At the same time the reward idea was being deliberately stretched past strictly decidable outcomes. The documented training-side extension is Rubrics as Rewards (RaR; Gunjal et al., July 2025): on-policy GRPO training in medicine and science where the reward is a structured, checklist-style rubric scored criterion by criterion, evaluated on benchmarks including HealthBench — whose physician-written rubrics (OpenAI, May 2025) supplied the design pattern. HealthBench itself is an evaluation benchmark, not a training method, and is cited here as the source of the rubric form, not as evidence of training adoption. RaR's rubrics are graded, partly by learned judges, which trades the strict verifier's objectivity for coverage of tasks where no exact check exists. Whether that extension is still RLVR is a live terminological question the literature has not settled.

3. Conceptual or Technical Framework
3.1 What RLVR specifies — and what it does not

RLVR is a claim about one component of an RL training system: the reward source. It does not specify the optimizer (PPO, GRPO, and variants are all attested), the advantage estimator (a learned value model, as in Tülu 3's PPO implementation, or a group mean, as in GRPO), or the starting checkpoint (base model or fine-tuned). Learned components may therefore participate in an RLVR training run — estimating advantages, anchoring the policy — without violating the definition, provided none of them supplies the reward. Conflating the reward source with the surrounding machinery is the most common misdescription of the method, and the source of the false impression that "RLVR" and "GRPO" are synonyms.

3.2 The verifier spectrum

RLVR systems differ less in their RL algorithm than in what their verifier can check. Five verifier classes cover the 2025 practice:

Verifier class What is checked Typical domains Reward signal Characteristic failure
Answer-match Final answer equals a known solution (exact or normalized match) Mathematics, factual QA with gold answers Binary Brittle to equivalent-but-differently-formatted answers; rewards the answer, not the reasoning
Execution Code is run; unit tests pass or fail Programming, tool-use with testable effects Binary or fraction of tests passed Test-suite overfitting; exploiting underspecified tests
Formal A complete submitted proof object is accepted by a proof assistant (e.g., Lean) Formal mathematics, verified software Binary Extreme domain narrowness; high cost per sample
Constraint Testable properties of the response: length, format, required/forbidden content Instruction following Binary or per-constraint Optimizes the letter of constraints; silent on quality
Rubric A grader scores the output against expert-written criteria Medicine, open-ended analysis, advice Graded/partial Reintroduces judge subjectivity — the proxy problem RLVR was meant to escape

Note on the spectrum's edge. The first four classes are deterministic programs and define RLVR strictly. The fifth is where the term is currently stretching: rubric graders are frequently themselves models, and several authors restrict "verifiable" to the deterministic classes, treating rubric reward as a neighboring method. This entry uses RLVR in the strict sense unless the rubric sense is specified.

3.3 The training loop

The generic RLVR training loop, abstracted from any particular optimizer, proceeds as follows:

  1. Start point. Begin from a base model (the R1-Zero setting) or, more commonly, from a supervised fine-tuned checkpoint that already follows instructions and produces well-formed responses.
  2. Prompt set. Assemble prompts whose correct outcomes are mechanically checkable — problems with known answers, coding tasks with test suites, instructions with testable constraints. Decontamination against evaluation sets is part of this step, since the training signal and the benchmark are drawn from the same kind of artifact.
  3. Sampling. For each prompt, sample candidate responses at nonzero temperature. Batches commonly contain many responses per prompt under both PPO-style and group-relative training; how responses are sampled and batched is an engineering choice, not what distinguishes the methods — the distinction lies in advantage estimation (step 5).
  4. Verification. Score every sample with the verification function: reward 1 (or +1) for an output that passes the specified check, 0 (or −1) otherwise. The reward comes only from the verifier (see §3.1).
  5. Advantage estimation. Convert rewards into per-sample advantages: against a learned value model's baseline (PPO, as in Tülu 3's original implementation), or against the mean reward of the sample's own group (GRPO, the form standardized by the DeepSeek line and DAPO), which needs no value model. Prompts on which all samples succeed or all fail carry no signal under the group-relative form and are typically filtered (DAPO's dynamic sampling).
  6. Policy update. Apply a clipped policy-gradient update (PPO-family; DAPO decouples the upper and lower clipping bounds), usually with a penalty or reference-model constraint limiting drift from the starting policy.
  7. Iteration with monitoring. Repeat, tracking pass@1 (the probability that a single sampled response succeeds), greedy-decoding accuracy as a separate deterministic measure, group pass rates, response length, and policy entropy; entropy collapse — the policy narrowing onto a few response shapes — is the standard early-warning signal.
4. Applications and Implementations

Named recipes:

  • Tülu 3 (Allen Institute for AI, November 2024) — the coinage and first fully open recipe: SFT → DPO → RLVR, over mathematics and instruction-following verifiers, implemented with PPO.
  • DAPO (ByteDance Seed, March 2025) — open large-scale system and algorithm variant; AIME 2024 score of 50 (avg@32) from a Qwen2.5-32B base with a bare ground-truth reward, as reported by its authors; the field's reference implementation notes.

Frontier demonstrations:

  • DeepSeek-R1 / R1-Zero (DeepSeek, January 2025) — R1-Zero: verifiable-reward RL from a base model with no preceding fine-tuning stage, during which long chain-of-thought and self-verification behaviors emerged in training; R1: the production multi-stage version. Nature 645, 633–638 (2025).
  • Kimi k1.5 (Moonshot AI, January 2025) — RL scaled with long contexts (to 128k tokens), explicitly without tree search, value functions, or process reward models; AIME 77.5, MATH-500 96.2.

Infrastructure:

  • veRL (ByteDance, open source) — the distributed RL training framework on which DAPO was built and which a number of the 2025 open reproductions adopted; its availability made RLVR pipelines inspectable and comparable across groups.
5. Evidence and Evaluation

The evidence for RLVR comes in distinct kinds, which this section keeps separate: reported benchmark gains by the systems' own authors, independent boundary probes, confound studies, and — the weakest kind — report-level evidence of diffusion.

5.1 Reported performance gains

The headline results are authors' reports under their own evaluation setups. Tülu 3 reported gains from its RLVR stage over its DPO checkpoint of up to +1.7 points on MATH, +3.3 on GSM8K, and +1.3 on IFEval [R1]. Kimi k1.5 reported AIME 77.5 and MATH-500 96.2 under its long-context RL framework [P4]. DAPO reported AIME 2024 at 50 points (avg@32 — mean accuracy over 32 sampled responses per problem) from a Qwen2.5-32B base, against 47 (avg@32) for DeepSeek's R1-Zero-Qwen-32B, reached in roughly half the training steps [P5]. What has been independently reproduced is the recipe class — open reconstructions by unaffiliated groups recovered RLVR-style gains — more than each individual headline number.

5.2 Capability-boundary evidence

Yue et al. [P6] compared RLVR-trained models with their own base models across several model families, RL algorithms, and mathematics, coding, and visual-reasoning benchmarks, measuring pass@k as k grows. At small k the trained models win; at large k the base models match or exceed them — evidence that, in the setups studied, training raised the probability of already-reachable solutions rather than extending the reachable set. This is the strongest evidence on the capability question, and it bounds, without refuting, the expansion claims made for R1-Zero-style training.

5.3 Confounds and sensitivity

Shao et al. [P7] showed that on Qwen2.5-Math models, rewards uncorrelated with correctness — random, format-only, or computed from incorrect labels — recovered most of the ground-truth gain on MATH-500 (+21.4 points from random reward, +24.1 from incorrect labels, against +29.1 from ground truth), via amplification of the family's pre-existing "code reasoning" prior; the effect was absent in the Llama and OLMo families tested. Measured RLVR gains are therefore unusually sensitive to the base model, and single-family results underdetermine method-level conclusions.

5.4 Status of the evidence

Consolidated: single-sample (pass@1) improvements from verifiable-reward training are robust across independent reproductions; the stronger claim — that such training creates reasoning capability beyond the base model — is not established and is actively contested (§5.2); effect sizes are base-model-dependent in ways the field did not anticipate (§5.3); and no systematic cross-laboratory survey of adoption exists, so claims about industry-wide diffusion rest on the report-level evidence of §5.5, not on measurement.

5.5 Diffusion, as reported

Report-level signals, stated as such, with two observations kept separate. First, demonstrated use of verifiable-reward training by independent organizations is documented in reports from Ai2 (Tülu 3), DeepSeek (R1), Moonshot AI (Kimi k1.5), and ByteDance Seed (DAPO), which do not share recipes. Second, adoption of the label is a narrower claim: the term originates with Ai2, and not every laboratory that used the method used the name — DeepSeek and Moonshot described their rewards in rule-based or outcome-based terms rather than consistently as "RLVR." The method's cross-laboratory diffusion is therefore better documented than the term's, and neither amounts to a measured survey of industry adoption. A related approach was productized for customers as reinforcement fine-tuning with programmable graders: OpenAI announced it in December 2024 as an alpha research program and made it available in May 2025 (o4-mini, for verified organizations) [R2]. Its graders span deterministic checks and model-based graders, so it is strict RLVR only in its deterministic configurations. Verifiable-reward training extended from mathematics and code into agentic tasks whose environments return checkable outcomes. And the critique literature itself (§5.2–5.3) became a named subfield within the year — a sign of a method being treated as infrastructure worth auditing.

6. Significance and Implications

RLVR's significance is partly methodological. It replaced the learned-proxy bottleneck of preference training with a specification problem: the reward became auditable code — inspectable, versionable, reproducible — rather than a neural network's fitted judgment. That auditability is a large part of why the method, unlike most frontier training recipes, was openly replicated within months: there was a definite artifact to replicate.

It was also the training-side engine of 2025's reasoning models, and it tightened a consequential loop: under RLVR, the training signal and the benchmark are the same kind of artifact (problems with checkable answers), which made evaluation design a training-relevant discipline and decontamination a first-order concern rather than a housekeeping detail.

Conceptually, RLVR reframed a central question of post-training. Preference training asks: what do humans judge good? Verifiable-reward training asks: what can we check — and what does the check actually measure? The year's characteristic failure modes and debates — verifier gaming, spurious rewards, the capability-boundary dispute — are that second question being worked out in public. The method's long-term importance may lie less in any single model's scores than in establishing measurement design as a core competence of model training.

7. Limitations, Debates, and Open Questions
  • Sharpening vs. expanding. The central debate. Yue et al. (2025) [P6], across the model families, algorithms, and task types they studied, found base models matching or exceeding RLVR-trained models at large sample budgets, and concluded that current RLVR concentrates existing capability rather than creating new capability — narrowing the solvable set even as it raises single-sample accuracy. Proponents answer that single-sample reliability is the quantity deployment actually consumes, and that longer training, curricula, and stronger base models may move the boundary in practice. The study challenges strong capability-expansion claims; it does not settle the question for all training regimes, and the debate was unresolved at the end of 2025.
  • Spurious rewards. Shao et al. (2025) [P7]: on some model families, rewards uncorrelated with correctness produce most of the measured gain, by amplifying pre-existing high-prior behaviors. The result does not generalize across families — which is itself the finding: RLVR outcomes are unusually sensitive to the base model's priors, and single-family results should not be read as method-level facts.
  • Verifier gaming. A deterministic check is not an incorruptible one: models learn to exploit underspecified test suites, answer-format loopholes, and constraint letter-over-spirit compliance. The reward-hacking problem is reduced relative to learned reward models, not eliminated; it migrates from the judge to the test.
  • Domain restriction. Strict RLVR reaches only what can be checked. Most economically valuable outputs — analysis, writing, advice, design — have no exact verifier, and the rubric extension that covers them re-imports the learned-judge proxy the method was defined against.
  • Entropy collapse and diversity loss. Group-relative training on binary rewards tends to narrow the policy's response distribution, improving pass@1 while degrading pass@k coverage — a measurable cost when the trained model is itself used for sampling, search, or further training data generation.

Open questions. Three define the method's next phase: whether verifier-grade signals can be constructed for open-ended domains without quietly reverting to learned judges; whether the capability boundary that pass@k studies locate in the base model moves with scale, curricula, or verifier-guided exploration rather than exploitation; and whether verifier design — writing checks that measure the intended capability rather than its easiest correlates — matures into an engineering discipline with its own standards, as prompt design did before it.

8. Related Concepts and Distinctions
  • RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback) — post-training against a reward model learned from human preference judgments. RLVR did not displace it: the two coexist, preference training remaining the standard where outcomes cannot be checked and RLVR taking the verifiable slice.
  • RLAIF (reinforcement learning from AI feedback) — the same architecture with AI-generated preference judgments; shares RLVR's motivation (escaping human-label cost) but keeps the learned proxy.
  • GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization) — the critic-free optimizer most often paired with RLVR; computes advantages against the mean reward of a sampled group. RLVR specifies the reward; GRPO specifies the update. Tülu 3's original RLVR used PPO instead — the pairing is historical, not definitional.
  • Process reward model (PRM) — a learned model scoring individual reasoning steps, trained on labeled step correctness; the road not taken by RLVR, which scores complete outputs only.
  • Rejection sampling (STaR-style) — generating samples, keeping those that verify, and fine-tuning on them; uses the same verifiers as RLVR but as a data filter rather than a gradient signal.
  • Reward hacking — optimizing the measured reward at the expense of the intended objective; in RLVR it takes the form of verifier and test-suite exploitation.
  • Pass@k — the probability that at least one of k samples solves a task; the metric on which the sharpening-vs-expanding debate turns.
  • On-policy distillation — training a student on its own sampled trajectories graded per-token by a teacher; the neighboring 2025 post-training method against which RLVR's capability claims are most often compared.
  • Test-time compute scaling — improving outputs by spending more inference compute; RLVR is the training-side counterpart that makes long inference-time reasoning worth spending.
